policy was corrected to match the app. RackUp is an app for billiards players who want to find an
opponent for a real game at a real venue. You post when and where you want to play, and another
player takes the table. This page explains, in plain language, what data the app handles, who can
see it, and what your rights are. Who is responsible for your data RackUp, Ioannina, Greece, is the
data controller for the personal data described on this page. You can reach us at
rackupbilliard@gmail.com about anything on this page, including any request about your rights. What
we collect, and why Your account To sign in you use an email address and a password, or Google
(Android), or Sign in with Apple (iPhone). Passwords are handled and stored by Google Firebase
Authentication and we never see them. If you sign in with Apple and choose to hide your address, we
store the Apple private relay address exactly as Apple gives it to us. Your profile record holds
your display name, your email address, the skill category you choose for yourself (Pro down to E),
your country and city, your usual hall and the games you play if you gave them, a link to your
profile photo if you have one, your notification preferences, whether you appear in player search,
your language, which version of the app you last used and on which platform, and a pointer to your
most recent venue check-in. Only you can read your own profile record. We use it to run your
account, to show your name and category on the matches you post or join, to build your player card,
described below, and to keep the games, halls, leaderboards and most notifications you get to your
own country. For an older profile with no city, the country is the app's default one. The app
version also tells us how many players have updated: together with the time your app last refreshed
its notification token, described under Notifications, it lets us count, as totals, how many of the
players who opened the app recently run each version. Week by week, we count how many players signed
up, by city and platform, and how many of them, in their first week, posted a match or a challenge,
asked to join a match, claimed or accepted a challenge, or said they were looking for a game. We
look only at those totals, never at a single player, to judge our social media posts and ads.
Looking around as a guest From version 2.3.0 of the apps, you can look around either app before you
create an account: tap Look around first on the welcome screen. The app then signs in to Firebase
Authentication without any details from you, which Firebase calls anonymous sign-in. That gives the
app on your phone a random guest identifier: a user id with no name, no email address, no password
and no profile. Firebase Authentication keeps it, with when it was created and when it was last
used. The app needs it because our database lets only signed in sessions read. Earlier versions have
no guest mode, and everything in this section applies from version 2.3.0. As a guest you can see the
open games in Discover, match pages, looking-for-a-game posts and the halls with their open games,
with each player's name, category and photo, and the hall, game and time. You cannot see what a host
wrote: the app shows the note on a match or a challenge, and the note on a cancelled match, only to
players with an account. On a match page a guest sees the line "The host's note is for players with
an account" instead. The note is also left out of a guest's Add to calendar entry. Versions 2.3.0 to
2.3.2 read the match and challenge records themselves and hide the note on screen, and there a guest
can also open a game that has been taken or called off, with the reason it was called off. From
version 2.3.3, the app reads only the copies of open games described below, which never hold a note,
so a game that has been taken or called off shows as no longer there. On every version, our security
rules still let any signed in session, a guest's included, read a whole match or challenge record,
note included, if it asks our database directly. You cannot open player cards or search for players.
Posting, asking to join, following, blocking, starring a hall, checking in, saving a search,
challenging a player and notifications all need an account, and the app asks you to create one
instead. Our security rules also stop a guest from creating a profile, uploading a photo, posting a
match or a challenge, or checking in. Copies of open games for guests. Our server keeps a copy of
each open match and each open challenge for guests to read. A copy holds the host's user id, name,
category, photo link and verified badge, the hall (its id, name, city, country and time zone), the
game and the time. A match's copy also holds the host's reliability figure, the hall's address, the
race, whether it is a play now post, when it was posted, and a yes or no for whether the host wrote
a note. A challenge's copy also holds when it was posted and the range of categories it is open to.
A copy never holds the note, a cancellation note, an opponent, a score or a no show. A copy is
deleted as soon as its game is taken, called off or deleted, and when its host deletes their
account. Otherwise our server deletes a match's copy within about an hour once the game is more than
2 hours past its start, and a challenge's copy within about an hour once it is 24 hours old or more
than 2 hours past its time. Any signed in session can read the copies, and from version 2.3.3 the
apps show them to guests. We keep no profile record, no player card and no notification token for a
guest. A guest can report a player from a match page, from a looking-for-a-game post or from a
challenge in Discover. The report is stored as described under Safety: reports and blocks, with the
guest identifier where an account's report carries its user id. A guest can also send us feedback
from the app. It is stored as described under Feedback, with the guest identifier where an account's
feedback carries its user id. When you choose to look around, Firebase Analytics records the event
guest_browse_started, with no detail, and from then on it records a guest's use of the app as it
does a player's, described under app usage analytics below, keyed on its own per installation
identifier, not on the guest identifier. When you create an account or sign in, you use that
account, which has its own user id. Your guest identifier is not merged into it. How long guest
identifiers are kept is under How long we keep it, below. Your profile photo You can set a profile
photo on Android and, from version 2.2.0, on iPhone. You pick it with your phone's own photo picker,
which gives the app only the photo you choose and no access to the rest of your library. The app
scales it on your phone to at most 512 pixels on its longest side, saves it as a JPEG and uploads it
to Firebase Cloud Storage as avatars/<your user id>.jpg. A link to it goes on your profile and your
player card. Any signed in player can view it, and from version 2.3.0 so can a guest, because it is
shown next to your matches and on your player card. Removing the photo in the app deletes the file,
from either app, and so does deleting your account. If a player's photo is offensive, you can report
them from their player card. Your player card and player search Each player with an account has a
player card, which our server has kept since 1 October 2026 and which other players with an account
can open from version 2.2.0 of the apps. It is a separate record, playerCards/<your user id>, which
only our server writes, copying from your profile: your display name, your skill category, your
country and city, your usual hall, the link to your profile photo, the games you play, whether you
appear in player search, and a yes or no for whether your app is new enough to receive direct
challenges. That yes or no is worked out from the app version on your profile; the version itself is
not copied. For search, the card also holds the beginnings of each word of your name, in Greek and
in Greeklish, without accents. Your card never holds your email address, your language, your
notification settings, your push tokens or your app version. The card screen also shows a player's
wins and losses this month, their win rate, reliability figure and verified badge, and the viewer's
own record against them. The viewer's phone works these out from match records that every signed in
player could already read; none of them is stored on the card. Player search is only for players
with an account. It finds a player when what you type matches the start of any word of their name,
in Greek or Greeklish, ignoring accents. You type at least 2 letters and see at most 20 players.
Email addresses and cities are not searchable. Before you type, it suggests players you played with
recently, taken from your own matches, and regulars at your usual hall. Players you blocked never
appear. Being findable is on by default. To stop it, turn off Show me in player search in Settings.
That takes your name out of search and you out of the regulars at your hall. Your card itself stays
readable: players you have played with can still open it, for example from their Played with
recently list or from a match's quick messages, and you still appear on your matches, challenges and
leaderboards. What you post and write The app is a noticeboard, so a lot of what it stores is
content you create on purpose: Match posts and open challenges: the venue, its city and country, the
game type, date and time, and any free text note you write, together with your display name, skill
category, photo and reliability figure copied onto the post. Every signed in player can read these.
That is the point of the app. From version 2.3.0 a guest sees them too, but not your note, which the
app shows only to players with an account; our security rules would still let a guest session read
it. Direct challenges: when you challenge one player by name, we store both players' user ids,
display names, categories, photo links and verified badges, the game type, the hall (its id, name,
city, country and time zone), the date and time you propose, any note you write (up to 500
characters), whether the challenge is waiting, accepted, declined, called off or expired, when it
was sent, answered and due to expire, and, once accepted, the match it became. Only the two of you
can read it, note included. If it is accepted, an ordinary match is created, which every signed in
player can read, but the note is not copied onto it: it stays on the challenge, visible only to the
two of you, and is deleted with the challenge, 30 days after its 24 hour answer window ends. Join
requests: when you ask to join someone's match, your name, category and photo go to that host. Only
you and the host can read the request. Quick messages: once a match is confirmed, its two players
can send each other messages from a fixed list of eleven, such as "On my way" or "I'm at table 4".
Nobody can type their own text. For each message we store the sender's and the recipient's user ids,
which of the eleven it is, the table number from 1 to 40 for "I'm at table", and when it was sent.
When you open the messages, we store a read mark for you, so the sender sees "Read" under the
messages you have seen. Messages can be sent until two days after the match's start time, and never
on a cancelled match. Only the two players can read them. Results and disputes: the scores either
player reports, whether the other player confirmed or contested them, no show reports, and whether a
no show was contested. These sit on the match record and are readable by signed in players.
Cancellations: the reason you pick and any note you add, on the match record. Post match ratings: a
one to five star rating and fixed tags you give an opponent after a confirmed result. A rating is
stored against the player it is about and only that player, and you, can read it. Venue check-ins:
if you check in at a venue, we store your user id and the time. There is no coordinate and no
location check, and since 27 August 2026 only you can read your own check-ins. A check-in counts for
3 hours, and our server deletes it within about an hour after that. Your profile record keeps which
hall you last checked in at, and when, until your next check-in. Check-in records written by older
Android versions may still contain your display name. Looking for a game: if you say you are looking
for a game, we store a post with the game, the day, the part of the day and either a hall or just
your city, with its country, together with your display name, skill category and photo. Every signed
in player can read it, and from version 2.3.0 so can a guest. You can have one at a time. It is
deleted when you withdraw it, and otherwise automatically, normally within two hours after that part
of the day has ended. Players you follow: if you follow a player, we store whom you follow and when.
Only you can read your list, and the player you follow is not told. When they post a new open match,
you get an entry in your activity feed and a push. Saved searches, starred venues and your weekly
schedule: private to you. A saved search and a schedule slot also hold the country of their city or
hall; a saved search with neither holds the country on your profile. Note that an enabled schedule
slot posts a public match on your behalf at the time you set. Free text you type into a name, a
match note, a challenge note, a cancellation note or the feedback form is checked against a
profanity list on your device before it is sent. Safety: reports and blocks If you report a player,
from a match or from their player card for example, we store your user id, their user id, the match
or post in question if there is one, and what you wrote. Reports go into a collection that no app
can ever read back. Only we can read them, in the Firebase console, and we do so to act on abuse. If
you block a player, we store their user id in a private list of yours. Blocking is invisible to the
person blocked. It hides their posts from your feed and keeps them out of your player search, and it
hides their quick messages and direct challenges, which also stop notifying you. Feedback The
feedback form in Settings sends us your user id, the category you pick, your message, the app
version and build, and which platform you are on. Like reports, no app can read this back.
Notifications We store a Firebase Cloud Messaging token for each of your devices, Android or iPhone,
along with your language, the platform, the app version and build, and the time it was last updated,
which the app refreshes each time it starts, so we can send you push notifications about join
requests, match updates, direct challenges, quick messages, reminders, saved search alerts, new
games from players you follow and, for halls you starred, new games and check-ins there. With the
app version, that time also lets us count updates as totals, as described under Your account. You
control notifications with the switches in Settings and with your device's notification permission.
The Android app stores its token whether or not you allow notifications; the switches and the
permission decide what you receive. The iPhone app asks for that permission once, after you sign in
and finish the welcome tour, and stores a token only if you allow it, then keeps it until you sign
out, even if you later turn notifications off in the iPhone's settings; its notifications are
delivered through Apple's Push Notification service. Signing out removes the token for that device,
and deleting your account removes all of them. A direct challenge adds an entry to the invited
player's activity feed and sends them a push naming the sender, the hall and the time; the answer
adds an entry and a push for the sender. A quick message comes only as a push, showing the sender's
name and the message in your language, and adds nothing to your activity feed. The pushes for both
follow the Match updates switch. Crash diagnostics (both apps) Both apps include Firebase
Crashlytics. When the app crashes or hits a handled error, Crashlytics sends Google a report
containing the crash, your device model, your operating system version and the app version. We use
it to find and fix bugs. We never attach your account to a crash report. The app never calls
Crashlytics' user id function, so reports carry only Crashlytics' own installation identifier, which
we cannot match to a person. App usage analytics (both apps) Both apps include Firebase Analytics,
and it is on by default. The old version of this policy said the app had no analytics profiles. That
was wrong, and correcting it was the main reason for the version dated 4 September 2026. We log 24
events about how the app is used, the same on both apps, or 23 before version 2.3.0, which adds
guest_browse_started. Most of them record only that something happened. Here they all are, with any
detail an event carries: Signing in and the first run: sign_up and login, each with the sign-in
method (password, Google or Apple); guest_browse_started, from version 2.3.0, when you choose to
look around as a guest; email_verified; tutorial_complete, when you finish or skip the welcome tour;
home_venue_set, when you pick a home hall at its end; and notification_permission_result, with
whether you allowed notifications. Matches: match_posted, with the game type, whether it was a "play
now" post and the id of the hall; join_requested; request_accepted; score_reported; score_confirmed;
score_disputed; opponent_rated; h2h_opened and h2h_rematch, when you open the head to head screen
and when you tap its rematch button; and reputation_opened, when you open your own reputation
screen. Looking for a game: looking_for_game_posted, with the game type, the part of the day,
whether any hall will do and whether it replaced an earlier post of yours; and
looking_for_game_converted, when a match is posted from another player's looking-for-a-game post,
with the game type and whether that post would take any hall. Everything else: venue_checked_in,
with the id of the hall; player_followed; saved_search_created; and notifications_opened and
notification_row_tapped, when you open your activity feed and when you tap one of its entries. The
only ids any event carries are hall ids, on match_posted and venue_checked_in. A hall id names a
billiard hall in our directory, not a person. No name, no email, no user id, no match id and no free
text is ever attached to an analytics event. Firebase Analytics also collects its own standard
measurements, such as app opens and sessions, and it derives approximate geography from a masked IP
address. Where new players come from. On Android, Google Play passes Firebase Analytics the tags of
the link that led to an install, and when that link was tapped. The tags name a source, a medium and
a campaign, and we choose them by where the link appears: for example our website, our Facebook
Page, an Instagram link, an ad, a QR code on a poster or a match link another player shared. An
install from a search in Google Play gets Play's own tag instead. We read these only as totals,
never player by player: how many first opens of the app, and how many sign-ups, followed each kind
of link. That shows us which of our social media posts, ads and posters bring new players. On iPhone
the app collects nothing for this. Apple tells us only totals, such as how many downloads came from
each of our campaign links, from our ads on the App Store, or from another app or website. Analytics
is not linked to your account. The app never calls Analytics' user id function, so Analytics keys on
its own per installation identifier. One practical consequence, stated honestly: because these
records are not tied to your account, we cannot pick out an individual person's analytics records,
and there is no in-app switch to turn analytics off. Uninstalling the app stops collection. Location
Both apps can ask for your approximate location, never your precise one, for one purpose: showing
the halls and games near you first. The app asks only after you tap the card at the top of the
Venues tab that offers to show the venues closest to you first or, from version 2.3.0, choose the
Nearest sort there, and it reads your location only while you are using it. The iPhone app asks from
version 2.2.0; the Android app, through its coarse location permission, has asked since its first
versions. Your position is used only on your phone, against venue coordinates the app already has.
From the hall nearest to you, the app works out your city. It orders every list of halls and games
with your starred halls first, then your city, then everything else, nearest first, and it opens
Discover on that city. The app never sends your phone's position to us, and we never store it. On
Android, if you allowed location, the Venues map opens on your area, so Google Maps loads the map
around you. The city worked out from your position orders what you see and is never written to your
profile or to any post. If you save a search from Discover without changing its city, the saved
search keeps that city. If you say no, nothing else changes: the lists put the city on your profile
first instead, and the app works normally. Checking in at a venue is on the honour system and is not
verified against your location. The location linked to your account is what you give us. The city
you choose for your profile, which sign-up asks for, and the country it is in are on your profile
record and your player card, described above, and the weekly sign-up counts are taken by city. We
count them as approximate location, linked to your account. We also keep the halls you name
yourself: your usual hall, the halls you starred, which bring you their notifications (on Android by
signing your device up with Firebase Cloud Messaging, on iPhone through your account), your
check-ins, and the hall or city of your posts, challenges, looking-for-a-game posts, saved searches
and schedule, each described above. What we do not do No advertising in the apps. There is no ad SDK
of any kind in either app. No advertising identifier. The Android app strips the AD_ID permission
that Firebase Analytics would otherwise pull in. The iPhone app links the version of Firebase
Analytics built without advertising ID support, shows no App Tracking Transparency prompt, and does

data, and we do not pass it to advertisers or data brokers. No contacts, no health data, no

text chat. Players cannot type messages to each other. The two players of a confirmed match can send
